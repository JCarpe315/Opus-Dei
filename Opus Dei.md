# Opus Dei: A Comprehensive Research Document

## What This Document Is

This document is the product of extensive research into Opus Dei — the Prelature of the Holy Cross and Opus Dei — a Catholic organization founded in Spain in 1928 that has grown into one of the most influential, controversial, and secretive religious bodies in the world. What follows is not an opinion piece, not a defense, and not an indictment. It is a compilation of facts, allegations, official statements, court documents, journalistic investigations, and historical records, organized so that a reader with no prior knowledge can understand what Opus Dei is, how it operates, what has been alleged against it, and what remains unknown.

Every claim in this document is sourced from publicly available information: court filings, Vatican documents, newspaper investigations, books by journalists and former members, official Opus Dei publications, and financial records. Where something is an allegation rather than a proven fact, it is labeled as such. Where something is an official statement from Opus Dei, it is presented as such. The goal is completeness — not concision. If a detail matters, it is here.

## Part I: What Is Opus Dei?

### The Name and the Basic Idea

"Opus Dei" is Latin for "Work of God." It was founded by a Spanish priest named Josemaría Escrivá de Balaguer on October 2, 1928, in Madrid. Escrivá claimed that while on a spiritual retreat, he received what he described as "the unexpected eruption of a divine light" — the idea that ordinary people, not just priests and nuns, could achieve sainthood through their everyday work. A banker could become a saint by being an excellent banker. A mother could become a saint by being an excellent mother. A lawyer could become a saint by being an excellent lawyer. This was not a new theological concept, but Escrivá built an entire organization around it.

The core idea is called the "universal call to holiness" — the belief that every person, regardless of their state in life, is called to become a saint. Opus Dei members are expected to integrate their faith into every aspect of their daily lives: their jobs, their families, their social circles. They are not monks who withdraw from the world; they are meant to be "in the world but not of it."

### The Personal Prelature — What That Means

In 1982, Pope John Paul II made Opus Dei something the Catholic Church had never had before: a "personal prelature." To understand what this means, you need to understand how the Catholic Church is normally organized.

The Church is divided geographically into dioceses — each led by a bishop who has authority over all Catholics in that territory. A bishop in New York has authority over Catholics in New York. A bishop in Rome has authority over Catholics in Rome. This is territorial jurisdiction.

A personal prelature is different. It has jurisdiction over people, not places. The head of Opus Dei — the Prelate — has authority over Opus Dei members no matter where they live. An Opus Dei member in Tokyo, an Opus Dei member in Nairobi, and an Opus Dei member in New York are all under the authority of the same Prelate in Rome. They do not need to ask permission from the local bishop to operate in that bishop's territory. This is a unique arrangement that gives Opus Dei extraordinary freedom of movement.

The personal prelature was established by Pope John Paul II through an Apostolic Constitution called *Ut Sit* on November 28, 1982. It was implemented on March 19, 1983, alongside the new Code of Canon Law. The canons that govern personal prelatures are Canons 294–297.

### The Problem with the Personal Prelature

Here is where things get complicated — and this matters for understanding many of the controversies that follow.

Under canon law, a personal prelature is technically an organization for clergy — priests and deacons. Laypeople can "cooperate organically" with it, but they are not formally part of it in a juridical sense. This means that the vast majority of Opus Dei members — the laypeople — exist in a kind of legal limbo within the Church. They are subject to the Prelate's authority in spiritual matters, but they have no formal recourse or protection under Church law as members of the prelature.

This was by design. Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger — the future Pope Benedict XVI — was involved in crafting the legal framework. The result is that Opus Dei can exercise enormous control over its lay members while those members have very little institutional protection.

In 2022, Pope Francis issued a motu proprio (a papal decree) called *Ad charisma tuendum* — "To safeguard the charism." He transferred oversight of Opus Dei from the Congregation for Bishops to the Dicastery for the Clergy. He decreed that the head of Opus Dei can no longer be made a bishop — breaking a tradition that had existed since the organization's founding. He also required Opus Dei to submit annual reports to the Vatican instead of every five years.

Francis said publicly: "A form of government based on charism more than on hierarchical authority is needed." He also said, "I am a very good friend of Opus Dei, I love them very much and they work well in the Church." But the changes were significant. In 2023, he issued another motu proprio modifying Canons 295 and 296, further defining the prelate as a "moderator endowed with faculties of an ordinary" and emphasizing that laypeople remain fully subject to their local bishops.

In 2025, Opus Dei submitted a third set of revised statutes to the Vatican. They were rejected. According to reports, the current Prelate, Fernando Ocáriz, had been arguing that canon law was flexible enough to allow laypeople to be formally part of the personal prelature. The Vatican disagreed. The only canonical fix, according to experts, would be to create a separate association for the laity under the Dicastery for Laity and Family Life — something Ocáriz has reportedly resisted. A leaked meditation allegedly had Ocáriz telling regional vicars at Easter that he was "waiting for the pope to die."

## Part II: The Founder — Josemaría Escrivá

### Early Life

Josemaría Escrivá de Balaguer was born on January 9, 1902, in Barbastro, a town in the province of Huesca in northern Spain. He was the second of six children. His father, José Escrivá, was a textile merchant. His mother, Dolores Albás, came from a family of modest means.

The family suffered a devastating financial blow in 1915 when José Escrivá's business went bankrupt. They were forced to relocate to Logroño, a city in the La Rioja region. It was there, as a teenager, that Josemaría reportedly saw the footprints of a barefoot monk in the snow — an image that awakened his desire for the priesthood.

He was ordained a deacon on December 20, 1924, and a priest on March 28, 1925, in Zaragoza. He studied civil law at the University of Zaragoza and received his licentiate in 1927. That same year, he moved to Madrid for graduate studies. His father had died in 1924, so Josemaría supported his mother, sister, and brother by tutoring law students.

In Madrid, he took on pastoral care for the sick, the poor, and manual laborers. University students began to join him. On October 2, 1928, during a spiritual retreat at the Vincentian headquarters in Madrid, he claimed to have received what he later described as "the unexpected eruption of a divine light" — the inspiration to found Opus Dei. He was 26 years old.

### The First Members and the Name

Initially, the organization had no name. The name "Opus Dei" came from a conversation with his Jesuit spiritual director, Father Valentín Sánchez Ruiz, who asked how "that work of God" was going. The first member was admitted in June 1929 — a student from Zaragoza named José Romeo. The first woman was admitted on February 14, 1930. Escrivá claimed that during Mass, God showed him that Opus Dei was for women too.

The first center opened in 1933: the DYA Academy in Madrid, offering classes in law and architecture mainly for students.

### The Priestly Society of the Holy Cross

On February 14, 1943, Escrivá claimed another divine inspiration during Mass — this time, the juridical solution for ordaining priests specifically for Opus Dei. The Priestly Society of the Holy Cross was established. The first three priests were ordained on June 25, 1944: Álvaro del Portillo, José María Hernández Garnica, and José Luis Múzquiz — all engineers by profession. The ordinations were performed by the Bishop of Madrid, Leopoldo Eijo y Garay.

### Personality and Temperament

Escrivá's personality is a matter of significant dispute. His supporters describe him as a warm, fatherly figure with a deep spiritual vision. His critics — including some former members — describe him as ill-tempered, arrogant, and authoritarian.

Multiple sources, including historians and former members, have described Escrivá as having a difficult temperament. Some former members who testified during his canonization process said he was unworthy of sainthood. The Swiss theologian Hans Urs von Balthasar reportedly dismissed Escrivá's most famous book, *The Way*, as a "handbook for senior scouts" — though von Balthasar later clarified that many accusations against Opus Dei were "simply false and anti-clerical."

Escrivá's writings contain passages that have been cited by critics as evidence of an authoritarian streak:

- "Blessed be pain. Loved be pain. Sanctified be pain. . . Glorified be pain!" (*The Way*, 208)
- "No ideal becomes a reality without sacrifice. Deny yourself. It is so beautiful to be a victim!" (*The Way*, 175)
- "Obey with your lips, your heart and your mind. It is not a man who is being obeyed, but God." (*Furrow*, 374)

These passages are presented by Opus Dei as spiritual exhortations about accepting suffering and obedience to God. Critics read them as instructions for unconditional submission to human authority.

### Death and Canonization

Escrivá died on June 26, 1975, in Rome. His death was reported by Opus Dei as occurring at midday. (Gareth Gore's book claimed it was before midnight; Opus Dei's rebuttal corrected this detail.)

His canonization was one of the most controversial in modern Vatican history. Pope John Paul II canonized him on October 6, 2002, in St. Peter's Square before a crowd of over 300,000 people. It was just 27 years after his death — one of the shortest waiting times in Vatican history.

The controversy centered on several issues:
- The lack of Spanish judges on the canonization panel.
- The fact that Opus Dei doctors were the ones who certified his miracles.
- Allegations from former members about his temperament and behavior.
- The perception that John Paul II fast-tracked the process because of his strong support for Opus Dei.

In March 2026, journalist Gareth Gore met with Pope Leo XIV and urged him to reopen the beatification and canonization process, presenting documents that he claimed showed "potential irregularities" and proved that "Opus Dei's system of grooming, abuse and control had been personally designed by this supposed 'saint'."

## Part III: Structure and Membership

### The Five Categories of Membership

Opus Dei has a complex, tiered membership structure. Understanding these categories is essential to understanding how the organization functions and where many of the controversies arise.

**1. Numeraries**

Numeraries are celibate lay members who live in Opus Dei centers — communal residences owned or controlled by the organization. They are fully dedicated to what Opus Dei calls "apostolic work" — recruiting new members, running formation programs, and managing the organization's activities. They are expected to work in professional jobs outside the centers (unless they are priests), but their lives are entirely organized around Opus Dei.

There are about 25,000 celibate members total, both men and women. This includes numeraries, associates, and numerary assistants.

**2. Associates**

Associates are also celibate, but they have permanent personal, family, or professional obligations that prevent them from living in centers full-time. They usually live with their own families or in their own homes. They have the same spiritual duties as numeraries where applicable.

**3. Supernumeraries**

Supernumeraries are the vast majority of Opus Dei's roughly 90,000 members. They are married or single laypeople who participate in Opus Dei activities — attending retreats, receiving spiritual direction from Opus Dei priests, participating in formation classes — but they continue to live their normal lives, with their families, in their own homes. Their participation is expected to be compatible with their family and professional responsibilities.

**4. Cooperators**

Cooperators are not members. They are people who support Opus Dei through prayer, financial donations, or volunteer work. They have no juridical bond to the organization and are not subject to its internal rules.

**5. Numerary Assistants**

This is the most controversial category and the one at the center of the human trafficking allegations. Numerary assistants are women only. They are dedicated to what Opus Dei calls "caring for people and activities linked to the Prelature" — which means cooking, cleaning, doing laundry, and maintaining chapel linens in Opus Dei centers. They live under obedience, commit to celibacy, and their lives are entirely controlled by the organization.

In men's centers, the numerary assistants are called "the Administration." They are the only point of overlap between the male and female branches of Opus Dei. The separation between men and women is almost total.

### Gender Separation

Men's and women's branches operate as entirely separate organizations. There are only two points of overlap:

1. Opus Dei priests, who are part of the men's branch, give spiritual direction to women members.
2. The numerary assistants, who are part of the women's branch, perform domestic work in men's centers.

The protocols for interaction are extraordinarily strict. In men's centers:
- Doors between male and female areas are locked from both sides.
- Men must stay out of sleeping quarters and dining rooms when women are present.
- Men are never allowed in the kitchen while women are there.
- All notes passed between male and female sides are read by two people.
- Phone calls between men and women must be brief, and another person must be present.

Female numeraries eat separately from numerary assistants — the assistants wait upon them.

### The Contract and the Vows

Before 1982, members of Opus Dei took private vows of obedience, poverty, and chastity. These were not public religious vows like those taken by monks and nuns, but they were binding spiritual commitments.

Since 1982, the system has changed. Members now sign a formal contract or declaration. The Prelature promises spiritual formation and pastoral care. The member promises to seek holiness, carry out apostolic work, and remain under the Prelate's jurisdiction.

To become a full member requires a probationary period of at least 5 years. Opus Dei claims that members must express their consent at multiple stages — at least 8 written reaffirmations over a minimum of 6.5 years. Members must reaffirm their desire to remain at least 8 times.

Critics argue that this system creates enormous psychological pressure. A person who has invested 5–7 years of their life, who has cut ties with outside friends, who has given their salary to the organization, and who believes their spiritual salvation depends on staying, is not truly free to leave — regardless of what the contract says on paper.

### Governance — Who Is in Charge

The Prelate is elected by an all-male electoral congress. The congress has 156 members, 62 of whom are lay electors. However, lay electors are not eligible to become Prelate themselves — only priests can hold that position. Women can suggest candidates, but their vote is not binding. Opus Dei claims the women's vote has "important consultation value," but the power to elect the leader rests entirely with men.

The current Prelate is Monsignor Fernando Ocáriz, appointed January 23, 2017, by Pope Francis. He is the fourth Prelate after Escrivá.

Previous Prelates:
- Josemaría Escrivá (founder, 1928–1975)
- Álvaro del Portillo (1975–1994)
- Javier Echevarría Rodríguez (1994–2016)

Ocáriz is a theologian who taught at the Pontifical University of the Holy Cross in Rome. He was a consultant to the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith under Cardinal Ratzinger (future Pope Benedict XVI) starting in 1986.

## Part IV: Spiritual Practices and Corporal Mortification

### What Is Corporal Mortification?

Corporal mortification is the practice of inflicting physical discomfort or pain on oneself as a form of spiritual discipline. It has a long history in Catholicism — saints like Francis of Assisi, Ignatius of Loyola, and Thérèse of Lisieux are known to have practiced various forms of it. The idea is that by voluntarily accepting physical suffering, a person can grow closer to Christ, who suffered on the cross, and can overcome sinful tendencies.

Opus Dei has been criticized for promoting and, in some cases, requiring corporal mortification practices that critics say are excessive and harmful.

### Documented Practices

**The Cilice**

A cilice is a small spiked metal chain worn around the upper thigh. Opus Dei members who use it wear it for two hours daily, except on Church feast days, Sundays, and certain liturgical seasons. The spikes press into the flesh, leaving small prick marks. It is used by some celibate members — both men and women.

Opus Dei defends this practice by pointing out that it has been used by saints for centuries and that it causes only "a low level of discomfort comparable to fasting." Critics counter that wearing a spiked chain against your skin for two hours a day is not comparable to skipping a meal.

**The Discipline**

The discipline is a cord-like whip used on the buttocks or back, typically once a week. Members need permission to use it more frequently. According to some accounts, Escrivá himself was so zealous in its use that he "splattered the bathroom walls with streaks of blood."

**Cold Showers**

Most numeraries take cold showers daily, offering up the discomfort for the intentions of the current Prelate.

**Meal Mortifications**

These include drinking coffee without milk or sugar, not buttering toast, skipping dessert, not taking seconds, and not eating between meals.

**Sleep Practices**

Female numeraries sleep on boards laid on top of their mattresses. Male numeraries sleep on the floor once a week. Both sleep without a pillow once a week.

**Gender Differences in Practice**

Male numeraries are allowed to smoke and go to bars — ostensibly to recruit new members in social settings. Female numeraries may not smoke or enter bars. Escrivá reportedly believed that "women had passions that required more discipline to tame."

### The Da Vinci Code Effect

Dan Brown's 2003 novel *The Da Vinci Code* and its 2006 film adaptation portrayed Opus Dei as a murderous, ultraconservative secret society. The portrayal was fictional and sensationalized, but it had a real impact on public perception.

A 2008 ORB poll in Britain found that readers of *The Da Vinci Code* were four times more likely to think of Opus Dei as a "murderous sect." Seventeen percent of readers believed Opus Dei had ordered or carried out a murder, compared to 4% of non-readers.

Opus Dei was careful to avoid protests and boycotts of the film while taking advantage of the notoriety to increase its visibility. Jack Valero, an Opus Dei spokesman in the UK, said: "Since we were founded in 1928 Opus Dei has promoted the highest moral standards at work, spreading a message of Christian love and understanding, yet the Da Vinci Code has persuaded hundreds of thousands of people that we have blood on our hands."

## Part V: Recruitment Methods and Criticisms

### How Recruitment Works

Opus Dei recruits through what it calls "apostolate" — spiritual outreach. Critics call it targeted recruitment with sophisticated psychological techniques. The truth lies somewhere in the documented evidence.

**Documented Recruitment Tactics:**

- **Targeting the young and impressionable**: Recruiters focus on teenagers and young adults, particularly those who are spiritually seeking, socially isolated, or going through difficult transitions (starting college, moving to a new city, experiencing family problems).
- **Friendship as bait**: Recruiters are instructed to befriend targets first. The initial contact is not presented as a recruitment pitch. It is presented as friendship, mentorship, or spiritual guidance.
- **Social activities not advertised as Opus Dei events**: Potential recruits are invited to dinners, retreats, sports activities, or study groups without being told these are Opus Dei activities.
- **Expensively appointed houses**: Recruitment often happens in well-appointed residences in desirable neighborhoods — places that feel safe, welcoming, and aspirational.
- **Atmosphere of delight**: Former members describe an atmosphere designed to be irresistible — "laughter, whistling, macho behaviour and good-humoured raillery."
- **Targeting traditional families**: Opus Dei particularly seeks recruits from families that still live by traditional religious principles. They want "well bred and educated people."
- **The "whistling" system**: Children aged 14.5 or older can become "junior candidates" by writing a letter to the head of Opus Dei. This is internally known as "whistling" — the sound of the letter being sent. It requires express parental consent as a rule of prudence, but junior candidates are not formal members and continue living with their parents.

### Allegations of Pressure and Control

Former members and critics allege that once a person shows interest, the pressure escalates:

- **Love bombing**: Potential recruits are showered with flattery, attention, and admiration.
- **Gradual isolation**: Members slowly break ties with outside friends and family. Letters and phone calls are censored or monitored. Reading material is controlled.
- **Financial control**: Members give their entire salaries to Opus Dei. They must ask permission for even small personal purchases.
- **The "engagements"**: Members commit to Opus Dei through formal "engagements" — often without their parents' knowledge.
- **Mail scrutiny**: Incoming and outgoing mail is read by directors.

### The ODAN Network

The Opus Dei Awareness Network (ODAN) was founded in 1991 by Tammy DiNicola's mother after Tammy left Opus Dei. ODAN provides information and support to people who believe they have been harmed by Opus Dei. It documents concerns about corporal mortification, aggressive recruitment, undue pressure, environmental control, and alienation from families.

## Part VI: The Spanish Civil War and the Franco Era

### Surviving the War

When the Spanish Civil War broke out in 1936, Opus Dei was tiny — about 30 members, most living in the Republican zone (the anti-Franco, leftist-controlled area). Escrivá swapped his priest's cassock for worker's blue overalls, put on his father's wedding ring, and went into hiding. He took refuge in eight different houses and hid in a psychiatric sanatorium for a time. Four members were confined to prison. Escrivá spent months hidden in the Honduran consulate in Madrid.

He feared being killed like other clergy. Thousands of priests, monks, and nuns were murdered by Republican forces during the war.

### Post-War Expansion

Francisco Franco's victory in 1939 created what historians describe as "the perfect conditions" for Opus Dei's expansion. Franco made religious classes compulsory and encouraged religious orders to create student residences. The Catholic Church became a pillar of the Franco regime.

Escrivá wrote in a letter dated April 27, 1939: "I believe that we will have to bless the war." He and his deputy, Álvaro del Portillo, had contacts with Franco, who had expressed "several times" his "sincere desire" to help Opus Dei.

### The Technocrats

In the 1950s, several Opus Dei members joined Franco's government. The most notable were Rafael Calvo Serer and Alberto Ullastres. They helped modernize Spain's economy during the reforms of 1957–1959, shifting Spain from economic autarchy (self-sufficiency) toward liberalization and international trade. Ullastres pushed forward the Plan of Stabilization.

This earned Opus Dei a reputation as a conservative power broker. Critics labeled it a deliberate infiltration of the government; Opus Dei insisted that its members acted as individuals, not as representatives of the organization.

The dossier is complicated by the fact that some Opus Dei members opposed Franco. Antonio Fontán, an Opus Dei member, was a journalist who advocated for free elections and trade unions and was persecuted by Franco. He later helped draft Spain's democratic constitution after Franco's death. Rafael Calvo Serer also opposed the Franco regime from within Spain and later from exile in Paris.

### Documented Opus Dei Members in Franco's Government

- **Alberto Ullastres Calvo** (1914–2001): Minister of Trade (1957–1965). Pushed forward the Plan of Stabilization.
- **Gregorio López Bravo**: Minister for Industry (1963–1969), Minister of Foreign Affairs (1969–1973).
- **Juan José Espinosa San Martín** (1918–1982): Minister for Finance (1965–1969).
- **Faustino García-Moncó Fernández** (1916–1996): Minister for Trade (1965–1969).
- **Vicente Mortes Alfonso** (1921–1991): Minister for Housing (1969–1973).
- **Fernando Herrero Tejedor** (1920–1975): Secretary General of the Movimiento (Franco's political party) in 1975.

## Part VII: Finances, Real Estate, and Banco Popular

### The Scale of Opus Dei's Wealth

Opus Dei does not publish consolidated financial statements. Its assets are held through a complex web of foundations, nonprofit organizations, and affiliated entities in dozens of countries. What follows is a partial picture assembled from public records, investigative journalism, and court documents.

### Banco Popular and the Collapse of 2017

On June 7, 2017, Banco Popular — Spain's fifth-largest bank, with a 90-year history — collapsed overnight. It was sold to Banco Santander for the symbolic sum of €1 (about $1.20). It was one of the largest bank failures in European history.

The bank's chairman from 1972 to 2004 was Luis Valls-Taberner, a lifelong Opus Dei numerary. He died in 2006. His brother, Javier Valls, also had ties to the bank.

Journalist Gareth Gore, who was assigned to report on the bank's collapse, discovered what he describes as a "clandestine network of foundations and companies" funneling money from the bank to Opus Dei.

**The "Balance Sheet of International Cooperation"**

On his third visit to the Banco Popular archives in northern Spain, Gore found a document in an "unofficial" archive — a collection discovered in a mansion in the mountains outside Madrid where Valls-Taberner had lived. An archivist had been sent to recover this "stray" collection and found that someone had been there before to purge it — but had missed this document.

The document, titled "Balance Sheet of International Cooperation," linked Banco Popular to more than 60 seemingly innocuous companies around the world. During the late 1980s and early 1990s, tens of millions of dollars were sent all over the world. The records were kept separate from the official Banco Popular archives.

Recipients were in countries including Australia, Cameroon, Ireland, Nigeria, and the Philippines. Many of these companies ran "vocational schools" similar to those implicated in the Argentine scandal. Gore alleges this was a "vast operation to entrap these young girls and then traffic them to work in the service of Opus Dei all around the world — and all of it financed by Banco Popular."

**La Sindicatura ("The Syndicate")**

Gore discovered an enigmatically named group called "La Sindicatura" that held 10% of the bank when it went bankrupt — a stake valued at over €2 billion ($2.2 billion) at its peak. A few weeks after the bankruptcy, La Sindicatura's main company notified authorities of its dissolution.

Gore alleges it was a "fake" syndicate — a ruse where big shareholders' votes were double-counted (as individuals AND as syndicate members) to swing important votes and prevent accountability. According to a source identified as "Javier" (brother of a former executive), "it was all fake... they double-counted those of us in The Syndicate."

**The Foundations**

Valls-Taberner created three charitable foundations:
- Fundación Hispánica
- Fomento de Fundaciones
- Fundación Patronato Universitario

Opus Dei's rebuttal states that these foundations provided donations and grants totaling €208 million and loans of €557 million. They claim 90% of loans were fully repaid and 10% were still being repaid, with €62 million outstanding in 2023. They deny that Fundación Hispánica funded schools in Spain and note that a loan of €120,000 for a summer camp called "Las Cabañas" in 1977 was fully repaid.

Opus Dei denies owning any companies. They claim the institutions are "promoted by members" with "formative and spiritual orientation" agreements. They also claim that board members "renounced being paid" and that Valls-Taberner's donations were "personal donations."

Opus Dei spokesperson Yago de la Cierva stated: "To attribute to him the disappearance of the bank thirteen years after his leaving the presidency and eleven years after his death is not only absurd; it is also despicable."

## Part VIII: The Argentine Human Trafficking Case

### The Accusation

In 2022, a lawyer named Sebastián Sal — himself a former member of Opus Dei — filed a complaint on behalf of 43 women. Argentine prosecutors concluded that from the early 1970s until 2015, "people holding different positions within Opus Dei established a structure dedicated to recruiting at least 44 women, most of them girls and adolescents, to be subjected to living conditions comparable to servitude."

The prosecutors who reached this conclusion were:
- **Eduardo Taiano**, head of the national prosecutor's office
- **María Alejandra Mángano**, co-head of the Office of the Prosecutor for Human Trafficking and Exploitation

## Part XI: The Robert Hanssen Spy Connection

### Who Was Robert Hanssen?

Robert Hanssen (1944–2023) was an FBI agent who spied for the Soviet Union and Russia. He is widely considered the most damaging spy in FBI history. ### The Critical Question: Did Opus Dei Know?

Gareth Gore alleges in his book that Opus Dei knew Hanssen was spying but kept it quiet to protect its reputation and its ability to recruit from the Washington elite. This is one of the most serious allegations in Gore's book, and it is also one of the least substantiated by independent evidence. What is documented is that Hanssen confessed his espionage to an Opus Dei priest at some point and then stopped spying for a period — suggesting the priest may have been the one who learned of it. ## Part XVII: The Junior Candidates Controversy

### The System

Children aged 14.5 or older can become "junior candidates" by writing a letter to the head of Opus Dei. This is internally known as "whistling." It requires express parental consent as a rule of prudence. Junior candidates are not formal members. They continue living with their parents and attending their regular schools. They cannot live in any Opus Dei center. They receive spiritual and pastoral help "adapted to their age."

There is a terminology dispute here. Gareth Gore claims that Escrivá invented the category of "aspirants" to get around Church restrictions on minors. Opus Dei denies this, pointing to Canon Law — specifically Canons 233, 234, and 643 — which allow minors under 18 in novitiates and minor seminaries. Opus Dei argues that the junior candidate system is not an invention of Escrivá but is grounded in existing Church law.

### Allegations from Former Members

More than 20 individuals said they had been actively drawn into the group as children after 1982 — when the 18-year minimum age for formal membership was supposedly in effect. A dozen said they had witnessed Opus Dei targeting children in the past 10 years.

A former deputy director of a boys' club in Colombia said: "Everything is done with a plan to recruit. The ideal age when they put pressure on is around 14 and a half... They target the weak people, people who need something."

A former member from Ireland (2016) witnessed adult members going through names of individual boys and assessing how "predisposed they might be to join." This was "the trigger for my leaving Opus Dei."

A former member from the US (left 2024) said: "One thing that could be improved is to make [reforms] more official: 'This is how it used to be. This is what we're now doing, because we want to avoid that again.'"

A former member from the US "whistled" at 16 after a youth course in 2013: "I felt so much pressure that I had to [join] right away, that I couldn't wait." The organization took control of her finances — wanted bank statements every month, receipts for tampons and shampoo.

A former member from Kenya said: "I was encouraged to use the cilice and discipline from the time I was 15 years old... We had to hide them from our family and friends."

A former member from Spain (left 2015) said: "The experience of recruiting minors has been constant during the 42 years that I was in Opus Dei. Not just constant, but intense, planned, abusive and shameless."

A former member from Spain (joined at 14, left 9 years ago) said: "To suggest celibacy to a 14-year-old, how is that suitable?"

A former UK member (left early 2000s) said: "From my experience, 14 and a half was the minimum age to ask for admission... They want people to join before they start getting influenced: puberty and being a grown-up, going out with friends, having sexual relations."

### Corporal Mortification Among Minors

More than a dozen former members said minors were encouraged to adopt practices such as the cilice and discipline. The Colombian boys' club deputy director said: "Shortly after writing the letter, the director gives them to the boys. In other words, some kids start using them before they turn 15."

Opus Dei responded that use by minors "used to happen in the past," but they were "surprised" by claims it's ongoing. They say there is no "explicit rule" but a "change of mentality" took place decades ago.

## Part XVIII: Vatican Relationships — A Timeline of Tensions and Support

### Pope John Paul II (1978–2005)

John Paul II was the most supportive pope Opus Dei has ever had. He:
- Established Opus Dei as a personal prelature in 1982 (*Ut Sit*).
- Canonized Escrivá in 2002.
- Made the first two prelates after Escrivá (del Portillo and Echevarría) bishops.

### Pope Benedict XVI (2005–2013)

Benedict continued the tradition of making Opus Dei prelates bishops. Fernando Ocáriz had been a consultant to the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith under Cardinal Ratzinger (future Benedict XVI) since 1986.

### Pope Francis (2013–2025)

Francis took a different approach:
- In 2016, he did NOT ordain Fernando Ocáriz as bishop when Ocáriz became Prelate — breaking a decades-old tradition. This was a signal.
- On July 14, 2022, he issued *Ad charisma tuendum*, transferring oversight to the Dicastery for the Clergy and decreeing that the head of Opus Dei cannot be made a bishop.
- On August 8, 2023, he issued another motu proprio modifying Canons 295 and 296, further restricting the prelature's autonomy.
- Francis said publicly: "A form of government based on charism more than on hierarchical authority is needed." He also said: "I am a very good friend of Opus Dei, I love them very much and they work well in the Church."

Ocáriz responded with "filial acceptance" and began a worldwide consultation to adapt the statutes.

### Pope Leo XIV (2025–present)

Robert Prevost was elected Pope in 2025. He met with Opus Dei Prelate Ocáriz twice: six days after his election, and again on February 16, 2026. The Vatican described the atmosphere as one of "great trust."

On March 16, 2026, Leo XIV held a private 40-minute audience with Gareth Gore. The Pope addressed the Pontifical Commission for Protection of Minors that same morning about "vulnerable adults."

## Part XIX: Gaps, Anomalies, and Unresolved Questions

This document has attempted to be comprehensive, but there are significant gaps and unanswered questions that remain. These are not minor details — they are central to understanding the full picture.

1. **The Hanssen Allegation**: Did Opus Dei as an institution know Robert Hanssen was spying and actively cover it up? No independent confirmation exists beyond Gore's claim. The FBI's official history makes no mention of Opus Dei knowledge.

2. **The Honduras 2009 Coup**: OURs claims Opus Dei involvement, but specific names and roles have not been documented in available research.

3. **The Exact Financial Flows**: How much money actually moved from Banco Popular to Opus Dei? The specific dollar amounts beyond the €2 billion Sindicatura stake and the tens of millions in the Balance Sheet document remain unclear.

4. **The 60+ Companies**: The specific names of the companies in the "Balance Sheet of International Cooperation" have not been publicly disclosed.

5. **The ICES Outcome**: What happened to the 140 women who joined Opus Dei from ICES Argentina? Their individual stories are largely undocumented.

6. **The McCloskey Second Complaint**: Opus Dei said in 2019 they were investigating a possible complaint from another woman. The outcome is unknown.

7. **The Real Estate in Europe/Latin America/Asia**: Beyond Villa Tevere, Strathmore, and a few named centers, the full global property portfolio is not publicly documented.

8. **The 2022 Internal Investigation Outcome**: Opus Dei Argentina's listening commission — what did it find? No public report has been documented.

9. **The Statute Rejection Details**: What exactly did Ocáriz propose in the third set of statutes that the Vatican rejected in 2025? The specific text is not available.

10. **The "Waiting for Pope to Die" Leak**: Ocáriz allegedly said this in a leaked meditation to regional vicars. The source is Antonio Moya/opuslibros.org — it needs independent verification.

11. **The Cipriani-Sodalitium-Opus Dei Nexus**: Did Cardinal Cipriani actively protect Figari and Sodalitium? Specific actions are not fully documented.

12. **The Bradley Foundation Opus Dei Affiliates**: Specific names of Opus Dei members on the Bradley Foundation board have not been documented.

13. **The Specific Vocational Schools**: Names and locations of the schools in Australia, Cameroon, Ireland, Nigeria, and the Philippines linked to the Balance Sheet document are not publicly known.

14. **The Gore-Pope Meeting**: The Vatican neither confirmed nor denied Gore's account of what the Pope said. Only Gore's version exists.

15. **The 10-Year-Old Recruits**: Gore claims children as young as 10 were recruited without parental knowledge. No specific documentation of this has been found beyond Gore's assertion.

16. **The Confessional Seal**: Gore alleges priests broke the seal of confession broadly. McCloskey's case involved absolving his victim after groping her — one documented instance, but the broader claim needs more evidence.

17. **The Women's Vote**: The non-binding nature of women's votes in prelate elections is a structural power imbalance that deserves further examination.

18. **The Argentine Case Outcome**: No formal charges have been filed. Judge Rafecas is still reviewing. The timeline is uncertain.
